This fall I am writing a directed study in economics with Prof. Onur Celik at Boston University. The question is whether women in Power 4 college basketball get less social-media engagement than men with the same on-court performance. I use Instagram followers as the measure of marketability and will estimate the gap with regressions in R. The department chair approved my proposal, and I am now drafting the introduction and literature review, so there are no results yet.

## The question

Since college athletes were allowed to earn money from their name, image and likeness (NIL), an athlete's audience has a price. If two players put up the same season and one has a much smaller following, she has less to sell. I want to measure how much of the difference in following between women and men is left once performance is held fixed.

The paper's broader working title is "The Evolution of the Gender Pay Gap in American Sports Compensation: From Title IX to NIL." The marketability question is the empirical part of it.

The plan is an OLS regression of followers on season statistics, a female indicator and their interaction, in levels and in logs. The coefficient on the female indicator measures the gap at the same performance, and the interaction shows whether better performance pays off in followers equally for women and men. ## What could be wrong with it

A gap in followers is not by itself a gap in fan interest. Women's games get less television time, and a player people cannot watch is a player fewer people follow. If coverage differs by gender, the estimate will mix audience preferences with media exposure, and I will need a way to separate them. [TODO: how you plan to handle coverage]

Performance is hard to compare across the two games. Points per game in the women's game and the men's game may not be the same unit, so a comparison on raw statistics could be off from the start. I have not settled how to put the two on one scale.

Followers also build up over years. A player may arrive at college with an audience from high school, which a single season's statistics cannot explain.
